# Overview

Lambert iGEM developed SWARM to protect honey bees from deformed wing virus (DWV) by targeting both the virus and the mite that spreads it, *Varroa destructor*.

We designed small proteins called minibinders to grab the virus's outer shell, and double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) to switch off genes associated with mite feeding, reproduction, and survival. Together, the two treatments target both infection and transmission.

# Introduction

Honey bees visit crop flowers more often than any other single species, and over 100 commercial crops rely on honey bee pollination in North America alone \[[1](#reference-1)\]. In our home state of Georgia, beekeepers lost about 88,000 colonies between October 2025 and June 2026, translating to ~20% of the state's colonies lost every three months \[[2](#reference-2)\].

Across the United States, beekeepers lost 55.6% of their managed colonies between April 2024 and April 2025 \[[3](#reference-3)\]. The importance of protecting honey bee populations extends globally, with animal pollination contributing an estimated \$235 billion to \$577 billion USD to crop production annually \[[4](#reference-4)\]. Although parasites, pesticides, and a lack of flowers all contribute to bee losses, the most significant threat to colony health is the parasitic mite *Varroa destructor* and the virus it transmits, deformed wing virus \[[4](#reference-4), [5](#reference-5)\].

<DwvMap />

The Varroa mite wedges itself beneath the overlapping plates of a bee's abdomen, piercing the soft membrane to feed on the fat body tissue underneath \[[6](#reference-6)\]. In doing so, it can transmit DWV directly into the bee, allowing the virus to reach levels associated with severe symptoms that are uncommon in the absence of mites, including deformed wings and premature death \[[5](#reference-5), [7](#reference-7)\]. As mites reproduce within developing brood, their populations grow, increasing opportunities for viral transmission and ultimately threatening the survival of entire colonies \[[5](#reference-5), [8](#reference-8)\].

**Table 1. Current Varroa treatments**

| Approach | Examples | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Synthetic miticides | Amitraz, tau-fluvalinate, coumaphos | Resistance has been documented; some miticides leave residues in wax \[[8](#reference-8), [9](#reference-9)\]. |
| Organic acids | Formic acid, oxalic acid | Formic acid can harm brood at high temperatures; oxalic acid does not penetrate brood cappings \[[8](#reference-8)\]. |
| Mechanical and cultural methods | Drone brood removal, screened bottom boards | Usually need to be combined with other controls \[[8](#reference-8)\]. |
| RNA interference (registered in 2025) | Vadescana, sold as Norroa | Targets one mite gene, not DWV; requires open brood \[[8](#reference-8), [10](#reference-10), [11](#reference-11)\]. |

As Claressa Lucas of the Metro Atlanta Beekeepers Association said, "It's not a matter of if there are Varroa mites. Every colony has them." (see [Integrated Human Practices](/lambert-ga/human-practices/)).

# SWARM

Based on these limitations and feedback from beekeepers, we established four design requirements for SWARM:

1. Target DWV directly, addressing the virus alongside the mite responsible for its transmission \[[8](#reference-8)\].
2. Develop an alternative to conventional miticides that could reduce mite populations while minimizing risks to brood health and wax contamination \[[8](#reference-8), [9](#reference-9)\].
3. Address miticide resistance by exploring RNAi as an alternative mechanism for controlling Varroa \[[8](#reference-8)\].
4. Prioritize affordability and ease of use by designing treatments compatible with existing beekeeping practices.

To meet them, SWARM pairs two treatments: minibinders against DWV, and dsRNA against Varroa.

## Minibinders

SWARM targets DWV with minibinders, small proteins designed computationally to bind to a specific target \[[13](#reference-13)\]. We designed our minibinders against VP3, one of the proteins that form the virus's capsid, or outer shell \[[14](#reference-14)\]. To validate our target selection, we contacted Dr. Benjamin Lamp and Dr. Tatsuhiko Kadowaki, whose advice confirmed the potential of VP3 as a target for interfering with viral function (see [Integrated Human Practices](/lambert-ga/human-practices/)). Previous research further demonstrated that antibodies targeting the viral capsid proteins VP1 and VP3 reduced DWV load eightfold in infected bees, providing evidence that targeting the capsid could help neutralize the virus \[[15](#reference-15)\]. However, conventional antibodies are relatively large and can be susceptible to digestive degradation \[[16](#reference-16)\].

We initially considered nanobodies because of their reported stability \[[19](#reference-19)\]. However, Dr. Aashish Manglik from UCSF advised us on the difficulty of computational nanobody design, stating, “For our computational design projects, we still make libraries of hundreds or thousands of candidates.” His advice highlighted the screening demands of nanobody design and encouraged us to look into minibinders (see [Integrated Human Practices](/lambert-ga/human-practices/)). After further consultations and literature review, we chose to pursue minibinders which were better equipped with computational design methods \[[13](#reference-13)\].

| Property | Antibodies | Nanobodies | Minibinders |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Size (in kilodaltons, a measure of protein mass) | ~150 kDa \[[17](#reference-17)\]. | ~15 kDa \[[17](#reference-17)\]. | Design-dependent; roughly 6–22 kDa for 50–200-amino-acid designs. |
| Thermal stability | Representative unfolding temperatures of approximately 65–85 °C. | Reported melting temperatures span approximately 45–85 °C. | Highly thermostable designs can have melting temperatures above 90 °C. |
| Origin | Based on naturally occurring immune-system antibodies \[[17](#reference-17)\]. | Derived from the binding domains of camelid heavy-chain-only antibodies \[[21](#reference-21)\]. | Designed computationally to bind a selected molecular target \[[13](#reference-13)\]. |

**Table 2. Comparison of antibodies, nanobodies, and de novo minibinders based on their size, thermal stability, and origin.** Reported values are drawn from separate studies hence the broad ranges.

Designing a minibinder against DWV's VP3 protein still presented challenges. VP3 forms part of the viral capsid and includes an exposed, flexible P-domain, complicating the selection of stable and accessible binding sites \[[14](#reference-14)\]. Tudor Ștefan Cotet and Corey Howe from Adaptyv Bio, who graciously sponsored our minibinder affinity testing, recommended targeting specific binding hotspots rather than allowing design models to select sites indiscriminately (see [Integrated Human Practices](/lambert-ga/human-practices/)). We initially identified potential hotspots and generated 15 experimental candidates, but our first experimental round produced no confirmed binding success. Following feedback from an engineer on the Boltz team, which develops frontier AI models for protein structure prediction and design, we refined our target setup and redirected designs toward more experimentally relevant hotspots (see [Modeling](/lambert-ga/model/)).

To improve existing hotspot selection, we built PRINCE, a software tool trained on more than 5,000 experimentally validated minibinder designs and benchmarked against existing hotspot selection tools (see [Software](/lambert-ga/software/)). We used PRINCE to prioritize new candidate binding regions on VP3 and selected 20 re-designed candidates for experimental testing (see [Results](/lambert-ga/results/)). Through two rounds of iterative design and validation, we tested our new batch of binders and successfully developed what we believe to be the first experimentally confirmed minibinder against DWV (see [Results](/lambert-ga/results/))!

For experimentation information, please see our [Minibinder page](/lambert-ga/minibinder/).

## RNAi

SWARM targets the mite through RNA interference (RNAi), a natural process that cells use to reduce the production of specific proteins. Cells make proteins using instructions carried by messenger RNA (mRNA). During RNAi, double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) is processed into small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), which guide cellular machinery to matching mRNA. The machinery breaks down that mRNA, leaving fewer copies of the instructions available for protein production \[[22](#reference-22)\].

We designed siRNA sequences in-house to target three *Varroa destructor* salivary gland genes associated with feeding, reproduction, and survival: Vd-CHIsal, Vd-CHIBIN, and VdAV422 \[[23](#reference-23), [24](#reference-24)\]. While Norroa targets a single gene \[[11](#reference-11)\], we combined our sequences into one multiplexed dsRNA construct to investigate whether targeting multiple genes could improve mite control and reduce the risk of resistance. We used Sf9 insect cells as a model system to evaluate the gene-silencing mechanism of our RNAi approach (see [RNAi](/lambert-ga/rnai/)). For its intended application within the hive, we propose delivering dsRNA through treated bees, allowing mites to encounter it during feeding \[[12](#reference-12)\].

We discussed the ecological implications of reducing Varroa populations with entomologist Jeff Pettis. He explained that it would be “completely fine to eliminate Varroa, as honeybees get no benefit at all from it” (see [Integrated Human Practices–Jeff Pettis](/lambert-ga/human-practices/)). His feedback supported our focus on mite control, while the safety of the proposed treatment is addressed separately on our [Safety page](/lambert-ga/safety/).

## Delivery

SWARM’s proposed delivery builds on feeding practices that beekeepers already use. The dsRNA would be supplied in a syrup pouch placed in the brood box, following the format used by Norroa \[[8](#reference-8), [11](#reference-11)\]. Freeze-dried minibinders would be mixed into syrup and administered through a standard in-hive feeder \[[26](#reference-26)\]. For the full delivery methodology, see [Implementation](/lambert-ga/implementation/).

# Impact and Sustainable Development

SWARM reflects our concern for honey bee health and the people whose livelihoods depend on pollination. We wanted this concern to shape both the treatments we explored and the opportunities we created for others to participate in science and pollinator conservation. Our Roots-to-Fruits model connects these efforts with seven United Nations Sustainable Development Goals \[[27](#reference-27)\].

Developing a useful treatment meant listening to the people who would use it. Beekeepers helped us understand practical treatment needs, while researchers informed our design decisions. These partnerships reinforced our emphasis on accessibility and compatibility with existing hive routines, putting Partnerships for the Goals (SDG 17) into practice. Our longer-term aim is to help protect agricultural pollination and reduce the financial burden of colony losses, connecting our research to Zero Hunger (SDG 2) and Decent Work and Economic Growth (SDG 8).

We also wanted community members to understand pollinator health and find ways to support it themselves. Our school workshops introduced students to the challenges facing pollinators, while our Concrete Jungle workshop helped volunteers choose pollinator-friendly plants. Together, these efforts connected learning with practical opportunities to care for local environments, supporting Quality Education (SDG 4), Sustainable Cities and Communities (SDG 11), and Life on Land (SDG 15).

Making science education accessible was equally important to us. Through Flowers for the Future, we continued into our third semester of teaching introductory synthetic biology to young women in Afghanistan, sustaining educational opportunities that support Gender Equality (SDG 5). Our Neurodivergent Handbook extends this commitment by giving educators guidance for starting their own Exceptional Science programs. By documenting and sharing our teaching approaches, we aimed to help others continue this work beyond our team’s direct involvement (see [Education](/lambert-ga/education/) and [Inclusivity](/lambert-ga/inclusivity/)).
