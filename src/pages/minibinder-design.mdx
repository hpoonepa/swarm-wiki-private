# Minibinder Design



Protein binders can be developed through experimental screening or computational design. Traditional methods, such as yeast surface display and phage display, involve screening large libraries of protein variants to identify potential binders. However, these approaches can be expensive and require specialized laboratory infrastructure. Alternatively, de novo computational design uses generative models to create novel protein structures and sequences tailored to specific molecular targets, reducing the need for extensive experimental screening (Cao et al., 2022; Pacesa et al., 2025).

## Justification

Lambert iGEM selected de novo design because large-scale library screening exceeded our laboratory's available resources and budget. Computational design allowed us to generate and evaluate numerous candidates *in silico*, narrowing them to a smaller set for experimental testing through our partnership with Adaptyv. This approach also enabled us to explore novel binding interfaces on the DWV VP3 P-domain without relying on previously characterized binding motifs.

## Computational Design

We utilized three computational protein design frameworks, **Mosaic**, **BindCraft**, and **BoltzGen**, to generate minibinders targeting the VP3 P-domain. Each utilizes a different approach to protein generation:

| Framework | Approach | Reference |
|:-------------|:---------|:----------|
| Mosaic | Modular framework combining structural and sequence-based optimization objectives | Escalante Bio |
| BindCraft | AlphaFold2-based hallucination with iterative sequence optimization | Pacesa et al., 2025 |
| BoltzGen | All-atom diffusion model that jointly generates sequence and structure | Stark et al., 2025 |

We incorporated binding hotspots identified using our software PRINCE to guide design toward selected regions of VP3 (See [Software](https://2026.igem.wiki/lambert-ga/software/)).

Following generation, we independently evaluated candidate minibinders using **Protenix and Boltz-2**, which predict the three-dimensional structures of protein complexes. We screened candidates using interface predicted template modeling scores (ipTM), which estimate confidence in the relative positioning of interacting proteins, and interface predicted aligned error (iPAE), which measures uncertainty in the predicted binding interface. These metrics helped us prioritize candidates with more confident predicted interactions.

We additionally used **SolubleMPNN**, a sequence-design model trained on soluble proteins, to redesign candidate amino acid sequences while seeking to preserve their intended structures and binding interfaces (Goverde et al., 2024). This step aimed to improve sequence properties associated with solubility and experimental expression without compromising predicted binding.

Together, these computational stages enabled us to generate, refine, and screen minibinder candidates before selecting promising sequences for experimental validation.
