# SDG 8: Decent Work and Economic Growth

SWARM aligns with United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 8, which promotes sustained, inclusive, and sustainable economic growth. Honey bee colony losses threaten the livelihoods of commercial beekeepers and the agricultural industries dependent on pollination services. By developing an innovative approach to managing Varroa mites and Deformed Wing Virus, SWARM aims to improve colony productivity and strengthen the economic stability of beekeeping operations. Through biotechnology innovation and strategic industry partnerships, SWARM also seeks to support sustainable growth within the agricultural sector.

# 1. Executive Summary

Honey bee populations face growing threats from *Varroa destructor* mites and Deformed Wing Virus (DWV), which contribute to widespread colony losses, reduced agricultural productivity, and financial challenges for commercial beekeepers. Existing treatment methods primarily target Varroa infestations, but increasing chemical resistance and the lack of direct DWV antiviral treatments leave a significant gap in colony protection. To address this issue, Lambert iGEM developed SWARM, a dual-action biotechnology platform combining engineered minibinders designed to inhibit DWV infection with multiplexed RNA interference (RNAi) targeting essential functions in Varroa mites. By addressing both the parasite and the virus it transmits, SWARM aims to provide a more comprehensive approach to honey bee health management.

SWARM enters a global apiculture market projected to reach approximately \$13.11 billion in 2026, with an initial focus on commercial beekeepers supporting high-value agricultural pollination. Our business-to-business (B2B) model prioritizes licensing agreements with established agricultural biotechnology and bee-treatment manufacturers, leveraging their existing production capabilities and distribution networks. In addition to SWARM, our team developed Vapor-X, a compact, low-cost vacuum-assisted drying system with less than \$80 in initial component expenses, presenting a separate commercialization opportunity for educational and research laboratories.

Our financial projections estimate a selling price of \$18 per hive treatment, with material costs of approximately \$5.70 per unit and a long-term production target of one million annual treatments by 2033. At this volume, SWARM could generate \$18 million in annual product sales before operating expenses and licensing arrangements. Our four-year development strategy emphasizes experimental validation, product optimization, regulatory preparation, field testing, and strategic partnerships. Through this phased approach, Lambert iGEM aims to establish SWARM as a commercially viable bee-health technology that supports sustainable beekeeping, strengthens agricultural pollination, and addresses an increasingly urgent environmental challenge.

# 2. Problem and Solution

## 2.1 Issue

The Varroa mite (see Figure 1), a parasite that spreads Deformed Wing Virus (DWV), is one of the greatest threats to honey bees. DWV weakens the colonies that beekeepers and farmers rely on for income and crop pollination, and it is spread by Varroa mites that feed on developing and adult honey bees. The virus can impair wing development, shorten bee lifespans, and weaken colonies. Together, Varroa and DWV can contribute to severe colony decline and even collapse (see Figure 2).

<Image
  src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6185/REPLACE-figure-2-collapse-feedback-loop"
  caption="Figure 2. Feedback loop of honey bee colony collapse."
  size="lg"
/>

This critical issue is the focus of SWARM because beekeepers must manage Varroa throughout the year rather than through a single treatment. Mite populations can recover after treatment, and untreated populations can grow quickly within a colony. Beekeepers therefore monitor mite levels and apply treatments at different points in the season to keep populations below damaging levels.

## 2.2 Burden of Varroa Mites

Lambert iGEM identified Varroa mites and Deformed Wing Virus (DWV) as a persistent challenge because beekeepers must manage both an aggressive parasite and the viruses it spreads. U.S. beekeepers lost an estimated 44.6% of managed honey bee colonies during the 2024–2024 season, the highest annual loss recorded by the national survey at that time (Giacobino et al., 2024).

Current control methods also face growing limitations because repeated chemical use can select for resistant Varroa populations and reduce treatment effectiveness over time (Bertola & Mutinelli, 2024). At the same time, DWV can remain in infected bees without obvious physical symptoms, allowing the virus to spread before beekeepers recognize a problem (Gauthier et al., 2007). Varroa further intensifies this issue by acting as a major vector for DWV transmission between honey bees (Martin & Brettell, 2019). Together, treatment resistance, hidden viral spread, and continued colony loss create a clear gap in current Varroa and DWV management.

## 2.3 Solution

Varroa mites are one of the leading causes of colony loss, creating recurring costs for beekeepers, and current solutions face resistance and inconsistent effectiveness. To address this gap, we created SWARM, a dual-sided platform combining a minibinder therapeutic targeting Deformed Wing Virus with RNAi designed to disrupt critical functions in Varroa salivary glands. By targeting both the mite itself and one of the major viruses it transmits, SWARM approaches the problem from two directions, aiming to reduce Varroa-related damage and improve long-term colony health. Since Varroa control requires seasonal treatment, the approach supports sustained demand and long-term profitability.

# 3. Market Analysis

## 3.1 Market Size

The global apiculture market is valued at approximately \$9.7 billion in 2024 and is projected to reach \$11.8 billion by 2030, growing at a compound annual growth rate of about 4.0%. Asia-Pacific currently represents the largest regional market, accounting for roughly 34.6% of global revenue. However, SWARM's potential market extends beyond beekeeping products because commercial honey bees are essential infrastructure for agriculture. In the United States alone, growers spent more than \$400 million on commercial pollination services in 2024, while honey bee pollination contributes more than \$18 billion in additional crop revenue annually. This creates a large downstream agricultural market in which maintaining healthier colonies can directly affect crop production, pollination availability, and beekeeper profitability.

We identified two distinct target markets for SWARM and Vapor-X, each addressing different needs within the biotechnology and agricultural industries. For SWARM, our primary target market consists of established agricultural biotechnology companies, animal health manufacturers, and commercial bee-treatment producers seeking to expand their portfolios with innovative pest and disease management technologies. Rather than distributing SWARM directly to individual beekeepers, our business model prioritizes business-to-business (B2B) partnerships, allowing manufacturers to license, produce, and commercialize our dual-sided technology through their existing manufacturing capabilities and distribution networks, following the necessary testing and regulatory approvals. SWARM's combination of RNA interference (RNAi) targeting Varroa mites and engineered minibinders targeting Deformed Wing Virus (DWV) presents an opportunity for companies developing next-generation pollinator health products.

#### Total Addressable Market (TAM): \$13.11 billion

The TAM for SWARM is the global apiculture market, which is projected to be approximately \$13.11 billion in 2026. #### Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM): \$400 million+

The SAM for SWARM is the portion of the market that our project could realistically serve after regulatory approval and product validation. In our case, this would be the United States commercial pollination and beekeeping market, especially beekeepers who provide pollination services to agricultural producers. The USDA reported that U.S. producers spent more than \$400 million on pollination services in 2024, making this a strong initial market for a bee-health product designed to reduce colony loss and improve pollination reliability.

#### Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM): \$364 million

The SOM represents the market SWARM could realistically capture first, given its current development stage, manufacturing limits, regulatory review, and adoption barriers. For SWARM, the most realistic early customer base would be commercial beekeepers serving the Pacific Southwest, especially California almond pollination operations, because this region has the highest pollination demand and the greatest financial incentive to protect colony health. The USDA reported that the total value of all pollination in Regions 6 & 7, which include the Pacific Southwest, reached \$364 million in 2024.

The SAM for Vapor-X represents the portion of the educational laboratory market that our technology could realistically serve based on equipment needs, institutional budgets, and distribution accessibility. #### Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM): \$34,000

The SOM represents the portion of the market Vapor-X could realistically capture during its initial commercialization period, considering manufacturing capacity, competition, product validation, and adoption barriers. Our proposed strategy involves licensing Vapor-X to an established laboratory equipment manufacturer that would produce and distribute the device to budget-constrained educational institutions. Assuming an initial three-year market penetration of 4% within our serviceable market, Vapor-X could reach approximately 117 institutions, generating around \$34,000 in retail equipment sales. This initial market would provide an opportunity to establish product demand before expanding distribution to additional educational and independent research laboratories.

## 3.3 Market Competition

The current market for SWARM is mostly made up of Varroa mite-control products, not DWV antiviral treatments (see Figure 5). These products can reduce mite populations, but they do not directly block the virus from entering bee cells or stop the DWV infection cycle itself. SWARM provides a dual-sided advantage by targeting both DWV infection and Varroa-mediated virus transmission. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) also notes that rotating Varroa products is important because resistance can develop, which makes SWARM's biological, multi-target strategy especially valuable (EPA, n.d.).

The current market for lab-grade vacuum dryers is vast, with prices ranging from \$400 for small laboratory benchtop units to \$118,800+ for large or specialized industrial machinery. However, small laboratories struggle to afford vacuum dryers: the least expensive options range from basic vacuum desiccators (around \$100 to \$250) for room-temperature moisture removal to compact benchtop vacuum drying ovens (starting around \$1,000 to \$1,300) for heat-assisted drying. These competitors (see Figure 6) provide advanced features such as precise temperature control, larger drying capacities, and specialized sample preservation capabilities. Vapor-X differentiates itself through its low-cost prototype design, with less than \$80 in initial component expenses, combined with automated temperature regulation, pressure monitoring, and a compact footprint. Although Vapor-X cannot perform conventional freeze-drying, its proposed value lies in providing accessible vacuum-assisted drying for suitable applications. Through manufacturing partnerships, Lambert iGEM aims to develop an affordable commercial product that addresses laboratory needs without requiring the investment associated with larger specialized systems.

# 5. Financial Analysis

## 5.1 Cost Analysis

For a full SWARM hive treatment, we researched material costs, but on top of these, there are other costs, including R&D, regulatory, marketing, administrative, manufacturing, and distribution costs (see [Implementation](/lambert-ga/implementation/)). To calculate total costs and revenue, we set our end goal to produce one million treatments a year by 2033. We plan to begin production in 2029 and scale by 200,000 treatments every year. SWARM would cost \$18 per hive treatment, which includes 4.0 g of multiplexed dsRNA targeting Varroa feeding genes and 0.5 g of the DWV minibinder, delivered in a sucrose matrix in a single peel-open pouch.

## 5.2 Revenue

SWARM's revenue is based on the number of treatments sold at \$18 per hive treatment, increasing by \$3,600,000 per year for the first five years and then peaking at \$18,000,000 after reaching our target production of 1,000,000 treatments a year (see Figure 10).

## 5.3 Production Costs

Pure material costs for our product come out to \$5.70 (see [Implementation](/lambert-ga/implementation/)). Full material costs are \$6.10, but due to bulk orders, we estimate a 40-cent discount. The two active ingredients account for most of this, at \$4.00 for the dsRNA and \$1.00 for the minibinder, with the remainder covering the sucrose matrix, stabilizer, pouch, and label. We estimate manufacturing costs to come out to roughly \$2 million per year, which accounts for facility space, utilities, packaging, quality release testing, and labor costs associated with production at our peak of 1 million treatments a year.

## 5.4 Miscellaneous Costs

In addition to manufacturing costs, there are other costs that must be paid either annually or once. To obtain the equipment and regulatory clearance necessary to produce our product, we estimate a necessary initial investment of \$5 million. This accounts for every necessary piece of equipment and for EPA registration as a new biochemical active ingredient, including but not limited to fermentation and purification equipment, lyophilizers, fill-and-seal lines, toxicology and non-target studies, and multi-site field efficacy trials. To remain competitive and grow our market share, we also need to invest in marketing, distribution, general administrative support, state registration renewals, and legal fees. This is estimated to cost \$1 million a year and will help us grow production by 200,000 treatments a year until we reach one million treatments every year.

# 6. Four-Year Business Plan

Lambert iGEM developed a four-year business plan to guide SWARM through experimental research and commercial implementation (see Figure 12). Our strategy prioritizes scientific validation, regulatory compliance, and partnerships with established agricultural biotechnology companies, allowing SWARM to enter the beekeeping market without requiring an independent manufacturing and distribution network.

## Year 1 (2027): Research and Development

Our initial focus will be validating the biological components of SWARM, including the ability of engineered minibinders to inhibit Deformed Wing Virus (DWV) entry and RNA interference (RNAi) to disrupt essential *Varroa destructor* functions. We will also refine our sugar-based delivery formulation and evaluate the performance of our automated dispenser and Vapor-X drying system. Initial funding will be directed toward laboratory materials, prototype improvements, and technical validation.

## Year 2 (2028): Product Optimization and Regulatory Preparation

Following successful preliminary testing, we will optimize SWARM's formulation, stability, delivery efficiency, and production costs. We plan to collaborate with university laboratories, apiculture researchers, and beekeepers to evaluate product feasibility and safety. In parallel, we will begin identifying the regulatory requirements for commercial use and assessing potential intellectual property protection. Vapor-X will undergo additional performance testing to evaluate its potential for separate licensing to laboratory equipment manufacturers.

## Year 3 (2029): Field Validation and Industry Partnerships

We aim to advance SWARM into controlled apiary trials, subject to regulatory authorization and successful safety testing. These studies will evaluate mite reduction, DWV outcomes, colony health, and practical application under varying environmental conditions. Alongside these trials, we will explore pilot-scale manufacturing and approach established bee-health and agricultural biotechnology companies regarding licensing or collaborative development agreements.

## Year 4 (2030): Commercialization and Market Expansion

Our primary objective will be to secure a licensing or technology-transfer agreement with an established bee-treatment manufacturer. Through this partnership, SWARM could benefit from existing production facilities, distribution networks, and regulatory expertise. Initial revenue may come from licensing fees and development milestones, with royalties beginning after authorized commercial sales. Market introduction will depend on demonstrated product safety, effectiveness, and the completion of applicable regulatory approvals.

# 8. Conclusion

Despite its major impact on honey bee colony loss, Deformed Wing Virus still lacks a strong and direct antiviral solution. Current treatments mainly manage Varroa mites, but they do not directly stop DWV from entering bee cells, leaving beekeepers with an incomplete solution. SWARM addresses this gap through a dual-sided platform combining DWV-blocking minibinders and a multiplex RNAi system targeting Varroa/DWV transmission. With a projected \$13.11 billion global apiculture market, over \$400 million in U.S. pollination services, and a \$364 million early regional market in high-value pollination areas, SWARM has clear commercial potential. If sold at \$10 per hive treatment, reaching 400,000 hives would generate \$4 million in annual revenue. With high market need and established beekeeper distribution channels, SWARM is positioned to become a scalable bee-health platform that protects colonies while supporting commercial agriculture.
