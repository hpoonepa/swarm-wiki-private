# Entrepreneurship

## SDG 8: Decent Work and Economic Growth

SWARM aligns with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal 8, which promotes sustained, inclusive, and sustainable economic growth. Honey bee colony losses threaten the livelihoods of commercial beekeepers and the agricultural industries dependent on pollination services. By developing an innovative approach to managing Varroa mites and Deformed Wing Virus, SWARM aims to improve colony productivity and strengthen the economic stability of beekeeping operations. Through biotechnology innovation and strategic industry partnerships, SWARM also seeks to support sustainable growth within the agricultural sector.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Honey bee populations face growing threats from Varroa destructor mites and Deformed Wing Virus (DWV), which contribute to widespread colony losses, reduced agricultural productivity, and financial challenges for commercial beekeepers. Existing treatment methods primarily target Varroa infestations, but increasing chemical resistance and the lack of direct DWV antiviral treatments leave a significant gap in colony protection. To address this issue, Lambert iGEM developed SWARM, a dual-action biotechnology platform combining engineered minibinders designed to inhibit DWV infection with multiplexed RNA interference (RNAi) targeting essential functions in Varroa mites. By addressing both the parasite and the virus it transmits, SWARM aims to provide a more comprehensive approach to honey bee health management.

SWARM enters a global apiculture market projected to reach approximately $13.11 billion in 2026, with an initial focus on commercial beekeepers supporting high-value agricultural pollination. Our business-to-business (B2B) model prioritizes licensing agreements with established agricultural biotechnology and bee-treatment manufacturers, leveraging their existing production capabilities and distribution networks. In addition to SWARM, our team developed VAPOR-X, a compact, low-cost vacuum-assisted drying system with less than $80 in initial component expenses, presenting a separate commercialization opportunity for educational and research laboratories.

Our financial projections estimate a selling price of $18 per hive treatment, with material costs of approximately $5.70 per unit and a long-term production target of one million annual treatments by 2033. At this volume, SWARM could generate $18 million in annual product sales before operating expenses and licensing arrangements. Our four-year development strategy emphasizes experimental validation, product optimization, regulatory preparation, field testing, and strategic partnerships. Through this phased approach, Lambert iGEM aims to establish SWARM as a commercially viable bee-health technology that supports sustainable beekeeping, strengthens agricultural pollination, and addresses an increasingly urgent environmental challenge.

## 2. Problem and Solution

### 2.1 Issue

*Varroa* mite (see Fig. 1), a parasite that spreads Deformed Wing Virus (DWV), is one of the greatest threats to honeybees. DWV weakens the colonies that beekeepers and farmers rely on for income and crop pollination, and it is spread by Varroa mites that feed on developing and adult honey bees. The virus can impair wing development, shorten bee lifespans, and weaken colonies. Together, Varroa and DWV can contribute to severe colony decline and even collapse (see Fig. 2). **[CITATION]**

This critical issue is the focus of SWARM because beekeepers must manage Varroa throughout the year rather than through a single treatment. Mite populations can recover after treatment, and untreated populations can grow quickly within a colony. Beekeepers therefore monitor mite levels and apply treatments at different points in the season to keep populations below damaging levels. **[CITATION]**

### 2.2 Burden of Varroa Mites

Lambert iGEM identified Varroa mites and Deformed Wing Virus (DWV) as a persistent challenge because beekeepers must manage both an aggressive parasite and the viruses it spreads. U.S. beekeepers lost an estimated 44.6% of managed honey bee colonies during the 2024-2025 season, the highest annual loss recorded by the national survey at that time (Giacobino et al., 2024).

Current control methods also face growing limitations because repeated chemical use can select for resistant Varroa populations and reduce treatment effectiveness over time (Bertola & Mutinelli, 2024). At the same time, DWV can remain in infected bees without obvious physical symptoms, allowing the virus to spread before beekeepers recognize a problem (Gauthier et al., 2007). Varroa further intensifies this issue by acting as a major vector for DWV transmission between honey bees (Martin & Brettell, 2019). Together, treatment resistance, hidden viral spread, and continued colony loss create a clear gap in current Varroa and DWV management.

### 2.3 Solution

Varroa mites are one of the leading causes of colony loss, creating recurring costs for beekeepers, and current solutions show resistance and inconsistent effectiveness. To address this gap, we created SWARM, a dual-sided platform combining a minibinder therapeutic targeting Deformed Wing Virus with RNAi designed to disrupt critical functions in Varroa salivary glands. By targeting both the mite itself and one of the major viruses it transmits, SWARM approaches the problem from two directions, aiming to reduce Varroa-related damage and improve long-term colony health. Since Varroa control requires seasonal treatment, the approach supports sustained demand and long-term profitability.

## 3. Market Analysis

### 3.1 Market Size

The global apiculture market is valued at approximately $9.7 billion in 2024 and is projected to reach $11.8 billion by 2030, growing at a compound annual growth rate of about 4.0%. Asia-Pacific currently represents the largest regional market, accounting for roughly 34.6% of global revenue. However, SWARM's potential market extends beyond beekeeping products because commercial honey bees are essential infrastructure for agriculture. In the United States alone, growers spent more than $400 million on commercial pollination services in 2024, while honey bee pollination contributes more than $18 billion in additional crop revenue annually. This creates a large downstream agricultural market in which maintaining healthier colonies can directly affect crop production, pollination availability, and beekeeper profitability.

We identified two distinct target markets for SWARM and VAPOR-X, each addressing different needs within the biotechnology and agricultural industries. For SWARM, our primary target market consists of established agricultural biotechnology companies, animal health manufacturers, and commercial bee-treatment producers seeking to expand their portfolios with innovative pest and disease management technologies. Rather than distributing SWARM directly to individual beekeepers, our business model prioritizes business-to-business (B2B) partnerships, allowing manufacturers to license, produce, and commercialize our dual-sided technology through their existing manufacturing capabilities and distribution networks, following the necessary testing and regulatory approvals. SWARM's combination of RNA interference (RNAi) targeting Varroa mites and engineered minibinders targeting Deformed Wing Virus (DWV) presents an opportunity for companies developing next-generation pollinator health products.

SWARM provides a dual-sided advantage by targeting both DWV infection and Varroa-mediated virus transmission. ## 5. Financial Analysis

### 5.1 Cost Analysis

For a full SWARM hive treatment, we researched material costs, but on top of this, there are also other costs including R&D, regulatory, marketing, administrative, manufacturing and distribution costs (see Implementation). To calculate total costs and revenue, we set our end goal to produce one million treatments a year by 2035. We would plan to begin production in 2031, and scale by 200 thousand treatments every year. SWARM would cost $18 per hive treatment, which includes 4.0 g of multiplexed dsRNA targeting Varroa feeding genes and 0.5 g of the DWV minibinder, delivered in a sucrose matrix in a single peel-open pouch.

### 5.2 Revenue

SWARM's revenue is based on the number of treatments sold at a cost of $18 per hive treatment, coming out to an increase of $3,600,000 per year for the first 5, and then peaking at $18,000,000 after reaching our ideal production of 1,000,000 treatments a year (see Fig. 10).

### 5.3 Production Costs

Pure materials costs for our product come out to $5.70 (Implementation). Full material costs are $6.10, but due to bulk orders, we estimate a 40 cent discount. The two active ingredients account for most of this, at $4.00 for the dsRNA and $1.00 for the minibinder, with the remainder covering the sucrose matrix, stabilizer, pouch and label. We estimate manufacturing costs to come out to roughly 2 million dollars per year, which accounts for facility space, utilities, packaging, quality release testing and labor costs associated with production at our peak production of 1 million treatments a year.

### 5.4 Miscellaneous Costs

In addition to costs of manufacturing, there are other costs that must be paid either on an annual or a one-time basis. In order to get the equipment and regulatory clearance necessary to produce our product, we estimate a necessary initial investment of five million dollars, which accounts for every necessary piece of equipment and for EPA registration as a new biochemical active ingredient, including but not limited to fermentation and purification equipment, lyophilizers, fill-and-seal lines, toxicology and non-target studies, and multi-site field efficacy trials. To remain as a competitive company and grow our market share, we also need to invest in marketing, distribution, general administrative support, state registration renewals, and legal fees. This is estimated to cost one million dollars a year, and will help us eventually grow to produce our end goal of increasing production by 200 thousand treatments a year, until we can produce a million treatments every year.

## 6. 4-Year Business Plan (See Fig. 12)

Lambert iGEM developed a four-year business plan to guide SWARM through experimental research and commercial implementation. Our strategy prioritizes scientific validation, regulatory compliance, and partnerships with established agricultural biotechnology companies, allowing SWARM to enter the beekeeping market without requiring an independent manufacturing and distribution network.

### Year 1 (2027): Research and Development

Our initial focus will be validating the biological components of SWARM, including the ability of engineered minibinders to inhibit Deformed Wing Virus (DWV) entry and RNA interference (RNAi) to disrupt essential *Varroa destructor* functions. We will also refine our sugar-based delivery formulation and evaluate the performance of our automated dispenser and VAPOR-X drying system. Initial funding will be directed toward laboratory materials, prototype improvements, and technical validation.

### Year 2 (2028): Product Optimization and Regulatory Preparation

Following successful preliminary testing, we will optimize SWARM's formulation, stability, delivery efficiency, and production costs. We plan to collaborate with university laboratories, apiculture researchers, and beekeepers to evaluate product feasibility and safety. In parallel, we will begin identifying the regulatory requirements for commercial use and assessing potential intellectual property protection. VAPOR-X will undergo additional performance testing to evaluate its potential for separate licensing to laboratory-equipment manufacturers.

### Year 3 (2029): Field Validation and Industry Partnerships

We aim to advance SWARM into controlled apiary trials, subject to regulatory authorization and successful safety testing. These studies will evaluate mite reduction, DWV outcomes, colony health, and practical application under varying environmental conditions. Alongside these trials, we will explore pilot-scale manufacturing and approach established bee-health and agricultural biotechnology companies regarding licensing or collaborative development agreements.

### Year 4 (2030): Commercialization and Market Expansion

Our primary objective will be to secure a licensing or technology-transfer agreement with an established bee-treatment manufacturer. Through this partnership, SWARM could benefit from existing production facilities, distribution networks, and regulatory expertise. Initial revenue may come from licensing fees and development milestones, with royalties beginning after authorized commercial sales. Market introduction will depend on demonstrated product safety, effectiveness, and the completion of applicable regulatory approvals.

## 7. Risk Management

The successful commercialization of SWARM depends on addressing potential scientific and operational challenges. As an emerging biotechnology-based treatment, SWARM faces uncertainties associated with experimental efficacy, formulation stability, and widespread adoption by beekeepers. To anticipate these challenges, we developed a risk management framework identifying potential obstacles, evaluating their likelihood and impact, and establishing mitigation strategies to support long-term commercial viability.

| Risk Category | Potential Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation Strategy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Technical | Computationally designed minibinders may demonstrate insufficient binding affinity or fail to inhibit DWV under experimental conditions. | High | High | Experimentally validate binding and antiviral activity, compare alternative epitopes, and refine computational designs. |
| Biological | RNAi treatment may produce insufficient gene silencing in *Varroa destructor*, reducing treatment effectiveness. | High | High | Evaluate target accessibility, delivery efficiency, and gene knockdown; optimize RNAi targets and assess sequence variability. |
| Product Stability | Minibinders or dsRNA may degrade during drying, storage, or reconstitution, reducing treatment effectiveness. | Medium | High | Conduct stability testing, evaluate VAPOR-X drying conditions, and optimize formulations and storage requirements. |
| Environmental and Safety | SWARM may produce unintended effects on honey bees, colony development, or non-target organisms. | Undetermined | High | Conduct off-target assessments, controlled toxicity studies, and colony-level safety evaluations before authorized field deployment. |
| Regulatory | Pesticide registration requirements and safety evaluations may delay commercialization. | High | High | Consult regulatory specialists, establish safety documentation early, and collaborate with experienced agricultural biotechnology manufacturers. |
| Financial and Operational | Manufacturing expenses, limited funding, or difficulties scaling production may restrict development. | Medium | High | Pursue research grants and strategic partnerships, evaluate production costs, and establish alternative manufacturing arrangements. |
| Market and Competition | Beekeepers may hesitate to adopt an unfamiliar treatment or favor established Varroa control products. | Medium | Medium | Conduct beekeeper interviews, compare treatment costs and effectiveness, and pursue licensing with established bee-health companies. |

## 8. Conclusion

Despite its major impact on honey bee colony loss, Deformed Wing Virus still lacks a strong and direct antiviral solution. Current treatments mainly manage Varroa mites, but they do not directly stop DWV from entering bee cells, leaving beekeepers with an incomplete solution. SWARM addresses this gap through a dual-sided platform combining DWV-blocking minibinders and a multiplex RNAi system targeting Varroa/DWV transmission. With a projected $13.11 billion global apiculture market, over $400 million in U.S. pollination services, and a $364 million early regional market in high-value pollination areas, SWARM has clear commercial potential. If sold at $18 per hive treatment, reaching one million annual treatments would generate $18 million in annual revenue. With a high market need and beekeeper distribution channels, SWARM is positioned to become a scalable bee-health platform that protects colonies while supporting commercial agriculture.
