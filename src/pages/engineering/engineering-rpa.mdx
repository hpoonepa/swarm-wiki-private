# Iteration 1

## Design
To enhance the sensitivity of our diagnostic, we utilized RPA, an isothermal amplification method capable of rapidly generating dsDNA copies that is well-suited for point-of-care diagnostics (Lobato & O’Sullivan, 2018; see Recombinase Polymerase Amplification). Because the dsDNA products initially formed by PDL are produced at a very low concentration, RPA amplifies the sequence to create a sufficient number for visualization on gel electrophoresis or for downstream quantification in our overall assay. 

To validate the feasibility of using RPA within our diagnostic, we first tested the commercial TwistAmp® Exo and Basic kits using a positive control provided by TwistDx (TwistDx, 2025b). Therefore, the goal of this first iteration was to ensure that the RPA kits could successfully amplify a known target before applying RPA to experimental constructs. The functionality of the kits was assessed with 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis with TBE (Tris-Borate-EDTA) buffer and fluorescent output on a plate reader, and controls included negative reactions with no template DNA to reduce the possibility of non-specific amplification. 

## Build
We purchased the TwistAmp® Basic and Exo kits and the positive control oligo mix and template DNA from TwistDx (TwistDx, 2025a; TwistDx, 2025b). Positive control reactions were performed according to the instructions by TwistDx as shown below, and we ran triplicates to confirm reproducibility (see Table 1).

# Iteration 1.2 – RPA Target Construct Experimentation
##  Design
Once we confirmed that our commercial RPA kits functioned as expected, we tested RPA on a synthetic target DNA construct that simulated the PDL product (see Fig. 1). To do this, we purchased a dsDNA RPA target construct ([BBa_25SSHRPA](https://registry.igem.org/parts/bba-25sshrpa)) which contained binding sites for all current and future primer sets. This allowed us to isolate the RPA process and first evaluate whether we could reliably amplify the target construct without confounding variability from PDL and actual protein samples.

For our experimentation with LANCET-specific RPA, we began with manually designed RPA primer sequences developed using the TwistDx® Design Manual (TwistDx, 2025). The guidelines for RPA primer design are:

**GC content:** 20-70%
**Tm (Melting temperature):** 50-100 ºC
**Maximum length of a mononucleotide repeat:** 5 nucleotides

We also intentionally positioned our primers on both aptamer constructs to facilitate amplification only in the presence of fully ligated products, rather than single aptamers (see Fig. 4).

To achieve this, we used the EZassay software to design multiple new primer candidates. From the software’s set of possible primer combinations, we filtered the results to meet specific requirements of our assay which EZassay’s algorithm did not account for. 

We excluded amplicons that were too short for our assay (\<100 base pairs) or those that did not encompass both aptamer constructs, which could lead to a false positive result (see Iteration 1.2 – RPA Target Construct Experimentation). # Iteration 1.4 – Conjugating RPA with PDL

##  Design
After confirming that RPA could effectively amplify a synthetic dsDNA construct, our next goal was to validate whether RPA could be directly integrated with the proximity-dependent ligation (PDL) step in the LANCET diagnostic workflow. This iteration aimed to test the compatibility of the two reactions and ensure that the dsDNA product generated by PDL could serve as a functional template for RPA amplification. By combining both reactions sequentially, we sought to demonstrate a continuous molecular pipeline from protein detection through PDL to DNA amplification through RPA. A successful outcome would show clear amplification bands at approximately 133 base pairs, corresponding to the expected size of the RPA product derived from the PDL sequence.


##  Build

We performed PDL using the optimized protocol established in Cycle 1, including incubation with anti-CspZ aptamers, bridge hybridization, and T4 DNA ligation. The dsDNA output from PDL served directly as the template for RPA using primer set 1.3, which had previously shown the highest amplification efficiency. Reactions were conducted using the TwistAmp® Basic kit, with negative controls including reactions lacking aptamers, ligase, or protein to confirm assay specificity (see something say something). All reactions were run in triplicates to ensure reproducibility.


##  Test

Experimentally, PDL-RPA product conjugations reported gel bands at the expected length of 133 base pairs for each amplicon, while negative controls showed no bands (see Fig. 8)

# Iteration 1.5 – Testing Protein Concentration Over Time

##  Design

To evaluate LANCET’s ability to function across various stages of Lyme disease, we designed experiments to test how different concentrations of the target protein CspZ—corresponding to 2, 25, 50, 75, 100, 150, 200, 250, and 300 days post-infection (pi)—affect amplification efficiency. Our goal was to confirm that the integrated PDL-RPA system maintains robust activity throughout the infection timeline, outperforming PCR-based amplification methods (see CspZ Protein Concentration).

Following the optimized protocol for PDL finalized in iteration 1.7, we also sought to evaluate how expected protein concentrations over multiple time points of simulated infection affected PDL efficacy. The goal was to determine the range of time for which our assay could reliably generate detectable dsDNA products and be compatible with downstream amplification.

##  Build
We prepared PDL reactions using expected CspZ concentrations corresponding to 2, 25, 50, 75, 100, and 150 days post-infection (pi) as shown below, while keeping other reagent concentrations consistent (see Table 1; see CspZ Protein Concentration).

# Iteration 1.6 – Experimentally Validating RPA Primer Sequences from CASPER

##  Design
To test the predictive efficacy of our in-house software CASPER, we selected primer set 1.5, which CASPER ranked as optimal for RPA amplification of PDL-generated dsDNA. The goal of this iteration was to experimentally validate whether CASPER-generated primers outperform our previous primer set 1.3 in terms of amplification efficiency and durability over time, using protein concentrations corresponding to 2–300 days post-infection (pi). Successful validation would confirm CASPER’s utility for designing high-performing primers for the LANCET workflow.

##  Build
Post-PDL RPA reactions were conducted using primer set 1.5 under the same optimized conditions as prior iterations. Negative controls included reactions lacking aptamer, ligase, or protein. Gel electrophoresis was used to visualize amplification, and ImageJ software quantified integrated density for each time point. Data were compared against previous experiments with primer set 1.3 and theoretical predictions from our deterministic RPA ODE model.

##  Test

Fluorescent bands at the expected 193 bp length were observed in samples with protein concentrations up to 250 days pi (Fig. 1). ImageJ analysis showed that primer set 1.5 outperformed primer set 1.3 across several time points and maintained detectable signal longer than primer set 1.3 (Fig. 2). Comparison to the ODE model demonstrated close alignment between predicted and observed amplification, indicating accurate primer performance (Fig. 3). Statistical validation using two-way ANOVA confirmed a significant interaction between primer sequence and time (p < 0.001), while a Tukey HSD post-hoc test further demonstrated that primer set 1.5 consistently produced higher amplification across time points than primer set 1.3 (Fig. 4; Table 1).

# Iteration 1.7 – Experimentation With Simulated Blood Serum

##  Design
To evaluate the robustness of LANCET under even more physiologically relevant conditions, we tested post-PDL RPA amplification using simulated blood serum spiked with CspZ protein. Primer set 1.5, previously validated for superior amplification efficiency, was used for all reactions. The simulated blood serum mimicked the pH and buffer composition of human serum without using actual blood products, allowing safe laboratory testing. Protein concentrations corresponding to predicted values over 2–300 days post-infection (pi) were tested to examine assay viability across the progression of Lyme disease.

##  Build
Post-PDL RPA reactions were conducted using the same optimized RPA conditions as prior iterations, substituting standard reaction buffer with simulated blood serum. Negative controls excluded aptamers, ligase, or protein. Gel electrophoresis visualized amplification, and ImageJ quantified the standardized integrated density of each band to assess amplification efficiency relative to standard in vitro conditions.

##  Test
Distinct 193 bp bands were observed in samples up to 200 days pi, demonstrating successful amplification in simulated blood serum (Fig. 1). ImageJ analysis revealed that amplification efficiency was slightly reduced between 100–200 days pi compared to standard conditions, but remained robust at all other time points and viable through 250 days pi (Fig. 2). One-way ANOVA confirmed no statistically significant difference in signal intensity between in vitro and simulated blood conditions (p = 0.432), supporting the assay’s functional reliability across conditions (Table 1).
