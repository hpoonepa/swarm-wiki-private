At the beginning of SWARM, we identified a severe lack of awareness about pollinators and bees in our community. Additionally, near the conclusion of our first Flowers for the Future semester, a student told us:

> "I had no idea what synthetic biology was, or if I wanted to do science at all in my future. However, through your course, I realized that I have a deep passion for synthetic biology and want to continue my education into college to become a scientist, or even a physician. Thank you so much for taking time out of your busy schedule to teach us synthetic biology despite it being difficult to reach us at times."

Our project's core is rooted in the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), guiding us to achieve sustainable education (see [SDGs](/lambert-ga/sdg/)). Seeing the impact that education has on students deeply inspired us to push past difficulties and gaps in knowledge to deliver informative lessons to anyone we could reach. Furthermore, our educational initiatives are designed according to proven research on STEM education, notably through our approach to inquiry-based learning and integration of real-world context (Teacher Strategies, 2025).

# Overview

We believe that for SWARM to have a substantial impact, our team must bridge the gap between Lambert iGEM and the broader community. To achieve this, we set our educational goals using a list of core principles:

## Quality Information

At the core of SWARM is our commitment to delivering lessons that provide students with meaningful knowledge and technical skills that will enable them to advance within the fields of biology. Through strong education, we want students to become confident problem-solvers not only within scientific fields, but also in their general understanding of the world. Additionally, we analyzed our students' engagement levels and participant feedback after every lesson. In doing so, we created a feedback loop that shows how our future lessons can be improved.

## Diversity and Outreach

Our goal is to ensure every student has the ability to learn and grow regardless of their age, gender, ability, or background. We aim to remove the barriers between background and education and help every learner achieve their potential. Because of this, our initiatives are not limited to North America, but extend to communities around the world. Through our inclusive education programs, Flowers for the Future (FFF) and Exceptional Science!, we are able to increase participation in STEM education and create meaningful opportunities across diverse student populations (see [Inclusivity](/lambert-ga/education/#inclusivity)).

## Hands-on Lessons

> "The traditional education framework, largely unchanged for decades, was originally designed to meet the needs of an industrial era. It focuses heavily on uniformity and compliance, producing graduates who fit into predetermined roles." (Dintersmith, 2018, p. 1)

We view education as more than just the transmission of information; it is about invoking thought and change within our audience. Instead of relying solely on conventional lectures and worksheets, we design every lesson to connect scientific theories with hands-on experimentation and inquiry-based learning. Our students are encouraged to question and investigate knowledge for themselves.

To achieve our quality education goal, we conducted over 30 educational initiatives, reaching over 530 participants from around the world. Additionally, we engaged with various stakeholders in the field of education for valuable insights and collaboration to refine our initiatives, ultimately allowing us to deliver meaningful lessons.

## Sharon Bees Workshop | SDG 4, 11, 15, 17

### Overview

We collaborated with the Sharon Elementary Critters Club to host an educational session about bees and pollinator gardens. In this workshop, we led engaging activities and discussions that taught our students about the importance of pollinators while they cultivated their very own garden. Notably, by letting our students work on the pollinator garden while we taught, we saw more enthusiastic engagement and curiosity about pollination than in traditional classroom lectures.

### Description

We started the lesson with a survey on common bee misconceptions to help address the negative stigmas surrounding these pollinators. At a young age, many children have simplistic reasoning about the world, such as thinking that bees are aggressive and dangerous because they have stingers and can hurt you. It is important for us to clarify these beliefs as they grow up. Next, we moved on to the main activity of our workshop: walking students through our lesson about pollination and the dangers bees currently face as we guided them through preparing the plots for planting (see Fig. 3). This activity helped students learn about the importance of pollinators and ecological sustainability at a young age through a hands-on approach. Additionally, we introduced them to iGEM and the biotechnology pathway at Lambert High School to give our students a preview of what this field has to offer.

Then, we led an activity to simulate bees' daily lives before and after being infected by Deformed Wing Virus (DWV), the leading cause of bee colony collapse. First, we distributed pieces of yellow cotton around the garden to act as pollen and assigned students the role of honey bees with the goal of collecting them. We then timed and recorded the amount of pollen collected in 5 minutes. After that, we repeated the activity; this time, the students were only allowed to hop on one leg to simulate bees that have lost the ability to fly efficiently due to DWV. The total amount of pollen collected during the second trial was significantly lower than in the first trial, where students could walk freely. Through these results, we illustrated a real-life example of how a virus that affects a bee's ability to fly can negatively affect a colony and the pollination of an ecosystem.

### Feedback

At the very end of our lesson, we hosted a pop quiz, which showed us that the students could answer questions about what they learned more confidently and were even able to brainstorm solutions to simple problems on their own. Not only that, they expressed great interest in the DWV activity for letting them have fun and learn at the same time. Through this experience, we learned alongside our students that interactive lessons are more effective, which helped us shape our future educational experiences. Moving forward, we decided to implement similar activities and improve upon our lesson experience.

> "This was so fun, I love honey sticks!"

## Children's Book | SDG 4, 15

### Overview

To extend our outreach beyond the classroom, we wrote and illustrated a children's book, *The Mite-ty Adventure of Bentley the Bee* (see Fig. 4). Made for children ages 5–9, the story follows Bentley, a brave worker bee who sets out on a journey to find a cure for his hive.

### Description

Throughout Bentley's adventure, readers are introduced to the challenges bees face while learning about the importance of pollinators. We work to address common misconceptions that many young children have about bees: that they sting when you approach, and aren't useful beyond producing honey. We achieve this by presenting bees as friendly and helpful characters rather than something to fear. Readers not only learn how diseases can impact bee populations, but also gain a greater appreciation for the vital role bees play in our ecosystems. Our book is now featured in the Atlanta Botanical Garden's library as part of its pollinator education for young children.

Additionally, as part of our Sharon initiatives, we hosted a book reading session for 1st grade students at Sharon Elementary School (see Fig. 5). The students showed great interest in pollinator protection after being introduced to the book, with many acknowledging that they hadn't known these facts about bees before.

To further increase the impact of the book, we translated it into 13 languages, helping us overcome language barriers to deliver educational materials to all young children. Additionally, *The Mite-ty Adventure of Bentley the Bee* was designed and illustrated to be accessible to readers with color vision deficiencies. Our goal was to ensure this book could be accessible to all; by making it available to read online, we aim to make it inclusive to all types of audiences (see [Inclusivity](/lambert-ga/education/#inclusivity)).

### Feedback

Throughout the development process, we shared drafts with elementary school teachers and incorporated their feedback to improve the book's readability, educational value, and engagement (see [iHP](/lambert-ga/human-practices/)). We used this feedback to create many iterations, each improving on the last, to refine our book (see Fig. 6).

# Middle School

Middle school represents a pivotal point in the learning process, where students begin to engage more deeply with scientific concepts and develop critical thinking skills. At this stage, they move beyond basic concepts and actively engage with more complex ideas, making middle school the perfect time to introduce them to more challenging ideas in synthetic biology. It is often during these years that students set a foundation for their whole educational journey (Bishop & Harrison, 2021). We believe that by challenging them with engaging lessons, our students will be able to establish a strong understanding and be confident in their academics.

## Riverwatch Workshops | SDG 4, 15, 17

### Overview

Our initiative allowed middle school students to directly engage with synthetic biology through our three-week series of workshops, with each session providing them with foundational knowledge and skills in biotechnology. Our workshop was advertised to all types of students, regardless of their background knowledge and experience.

### Week 1

Many middle school students take a brief introductory course on biology; however, this course only covers the basics of cell interactions and completely leaves out synthetic biology. To address this, we decided to start by introducing synthetic biology and iGEM. We covered not only what synthetic biology is, but also examples of what it produces in the world and how it guides new research.

We also taught the students the basics of pipetting and data analysis. These two skills are essential to any experimentation in synthetic biology. We first taught them the mechanics of the pipette along with the common mistakes that lead to contamination, incorrect volumes, and broken pipettes. We then had the students practice pipetting onto miniPCR plates before having them pipette real DNA (see Fig. 7). Afterwards, we had students plant sunflowers and explained to them the significance of pollinator plants in the ecosystem and the dangers that come when they are depleted.

Slides: [Week One Genetic Engineering Workshop](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1rSMZkrXkkefkTw-qGYibAOC7HUXC4YEuKhgoK5ynETw/edit?usp=drive_link)

### Week 2

In the second session, we familiarized the students with the central dogma of biology and had them run hands-on labs. In the initial pre-survey, many students submitted questions about DNA and its processes, leading us to go more in depth in this lesson to satisfy their curiosity. The session focused on how genetic information flows from DNA to RNA to protein and allowed students to explore these concepts through experimentation. Many students had little to no prior knowledge of the central dogma or synthetic biology concepts. However, through interactive explanations and guided discussions, they quickly developed an understanding of protein production.

They demonstrated growth in both confidence and scientific reasoning, and learned to actively apply their newly acquired knowledge and formulate hypotheses during the experiment (see Fig. 7). Rather than just following instructions or looking up answers, students began predicting experimental outcomes based on their understanding of genetic processes, discussing how changes to DNA sequences might influence protein production. By the end of the session, students were able to explain the central dogma and use it as a framework for interpreting experimental results.

Slides: [Middle School Workshop Week 2](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1eIPq9FHaBH-lCZ3i3hUvdWQ2lOITN3o8FUN018-5zl8/edit?usp=drive_link)

### Week 3

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and gel electrophoresis are cornerstone experiments in genetic engineering and synthetic biology, so we felt that concluding the workshop series with these two topics was essential. We taught students how PCR works and how scientists test its results using gel electrophoresis. Afterwards, we ran a lab activity where students were tasked with using gel electrophoresis to test DNA samples from the "International Space Station" for the harmful bacterium *Klebsiella aerogenes*. This required them to recall the skills they learned in Week 1 to pipette DNA samples into gels. Once they finished, we helped them run the gels to see whether or not bacterial DNA was present (see Fig. 7).

Slides: [Middle School Workshop Week 3](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/17i_GYoRpfepeiZJ6JMUCKm8LaqRa-hoYXS3IqkHpR64/edit?usp=drive_link)

## Biotech Bootcamp | SDG 4, 15, 17

This year, we held the Lambert iGEM Biotech Bootcamp, a summer camp where students learn basic biology and synthetic biology concepts to prepare for a high school career in biotech. Each day of camp centers on an iGEM committee, from Human Practices to Hardware, giving our campers a well-rounded experience. By combining learning with engagement and creativity, we gathered up to 62 campers and gave them the chance to explore science in ways that are both educational and exciting beyond the traditional classroom.

### Day 1: Wet Lab

- **Candy DNA model activity:** We started with a basic DNA model activity to introduce campers to the structure of DNA and why it is needed for life.
- **Intro to measurements and micropipetting:** Campers learned about the importance of measurements and techniques often used in microbiology. They were taught how to use a micropipette properly and practiced pipetting small volumes after calculating them. Through this activity, our campers became familiar with one of the basic procedures used daily in biotechnology.
- **Strawberry DNA extraction:** Campers learned how to read and use a simple protocol and applied the skills they had learned to extract DNA from a strawberry.
- ***C. elegans* RNAi lab:** Our campers were introduced to a complex protocol on RNAi that is directly related to this year's project. Throughout the week, they learned how to induce RNAi by feeding *C. elegans* and observing them under the microscope (see Fig. 10). This activity allowed our campers to learn about more complex biotechnology techniques used in real applications.
- **Swabbing experiment, "What is the germiest place in school?":** This activity introduced campers to swabbing and culturing bacteria on agar plates. They were instructed to swab and culture the place they thought had the most bacteria in the school and try to identify it.

### Day 2: Human Practices

On the second day, we focused on Human Practices (HP), starting with what HP is and why it is important. Then, we introduced campers to this year's HP educational goals: Varroa mites and vector-borne diseases. Campers discussed and researched the impact of pollinators and their decline, analyzing how our community could be affected. Through this activity, campers learned about the importance of HP and how it connects our wet lab to the broader community.

- **PCR and gel electrophoresis:** Campers were introduced to PCR, how it works, and its applications. Then, we moved on to our lecture on gel electrophoresis. After explaining the theory and applications, we guided campers through setting up and running a gel, showing them the proper techniques (see Fig. 11). This activity let campers gain more experience and confidence handling a micropipette.
- **Protein lab:** Campers examined the primary, secondary, and tertiary structures of fluorescent proteins and predicted their functions. Then, they used a cell-free system to produce the proteins and test their predictions. Through this activity, they learned about the basic structure of proteins and how they interact with one another, solidifying their biological foundations.
- **Bioethics debate:** To get campers to consider the ethical side of biotechnology, we included a bioethics debate. Campers were encouraged to think more deeply about the nuances and implications of certain experiments and debate with their peers.
- **Zoom call with Dr. Sharer:** Campers had the opportunity to learn more about the jobs and positions available in medicine and genetic engineering.

### Day 3: Hardware

- **Introduction to hardware and frugal science:** We covered the basics of hardware and the importance of accessible science, and introduced campers to the engineering process they will use throughout their science journey: design, build, test, learn.
- **DIY invisible ink pens:** Campers were introduced to a practical use of fluorescence by making their own invisible ink pens. Then, they learned about the applications of fluorescence in synthetic biology.
- **Bristle Bots, convex lens phone camera microscopes, and whiteboard activities:** In these activities, students were introduced to basic hardware structures and concepts that they can build themselves. First, students were guided through making Bristle Bots, DIY "bugs" made with a coin battery and a vibration motor that vibrates the bristles of a toothbrush head to mimic the motion of a scurrying bug. The Bristle Bot served as a demonstration of hardware, letting campers see the endless possibilities of what they can design and create. Then, through a similar process, they built a makeshift microscope using a small convex lens attached to a phone camera. Finally, to build upon their experiences in past activities, campers came up with their own hardware ideas and shared them with their peers on whiteboards.

Slides: [iGEM Summer Camp 2026 Day 3 – Hardware](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/14QZ8rcWw_gP849z_us04qSoc78Ayl_0HvyCC18dyfzA/edit?usp=sharing)

### Day 4: Software and Modeling

- **Introduction to software:** Campers were first introduced to the basic concepts of software, why it is important, and its applications within synthetic biology. They were also introduced to many types of scientific equipment and their functions. Then, we introduced campers to modeling software, along with a demo of AlphaFold and Foldit. Campers then created their own models in Teachable Machine by classifying images, allowing them to build a basic model and visualize how it works.
- **Bee simulation activity:** We ran a simulation of DWV spreading throughout a hive and modeled it in Desmos, visualizing the curve for our campers. Through this activity, campers became more familiar with the basics of modeling in practice.

Slides: [iGEM Summer Camp 2026 Day 4 – Software/Modeling](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/19aNmPQFNh-hZg6R_xq8455dAE7Hj3WpfLtAhLTMnn2Q/edit?slide=id.g1ea99e6fb59_0_65#slide=id.g1ea99e6fb59_0_65)

# Inclusivity

Inclusivity is the act of ensuring resources and opportunities are available to everyone, regardless of nationality or social status. Historically, Lambert iGEM has worked hard to uphold this value, and 2026 is no different. Through programs such as Flowers for the Future and Exceptional Science!, and materials including our children's book translations and Neurodivergent Handbook, we have taken special consideration to ensure our initiatives have a positive impact across demographics.

## Flowers for the Future | SDG 4, 5, 11, 15, 17

Sustainable Development Goal 5 (SDG 5) promotes equal access to resources and education, regardless of gender. We believe gender should bear no weight in whether an individual is given the opportunity to succeed. To achieve this goal, we partnered with Flowers for the Future (FFF), a non-profit program where Afghan girls can continue their education and obtain their General Education Diploma. Under Afghanistan's oppressive regime, these students are unable to attend school past the sixth grade, making this program especially important in promoting autonomy and higher education. We are currently in the third semester of our course, Intro to Synthetic Biology, and have reached approximately 120 students to date. In our class, we teach several key concepts within biological science, including but not limited to:

- Central dogma
- Polymerase chain reaction and gel electrophoresis
- Experimental design
- Scientific writing
- Bioethics

### Week 1: [Introduction to Synthetic Biology & Basic Lab Skills](https://www.canva.com/design/DAHCWr88fbI/S-9khKty9_84UYdSq52hvg/edit)

In this lesson, students learn about what synthetic biology is and how they should conduct themselves in a lab setting. In addition, we teach them about our iGEM team and how we use synthetic biology (see Fig. 1).

### Week 2: [Understanding Units, Dimensional Analysis, and Lab Calculations](https://www.canva.com/design/DAGhoqA7vw0/uQ628NneXXZ7CUhGk3u2dw/edit)

Week 2 is about understanding how to measure data. This week, we teach students about the units used for scientific research and how they are applied in experimentation. We explore how scientists dilute stock solutions to achieve the desired molarity.

### Week 3: [DNA](https://www.canva.com/design/DAHEgVnnwZw/w5xfvXXiMZxXNI7z-75NYg/edit)

In Week 3, we introduce students to DNA and how it functions within the body. Students learn about base pair interactions, mutations, and how the two can affect research. This week is designed to prepare students for future discussions about modifying gene expression in organisms.

### Week 4: [Gene Expression & Protein Production](https://canva.link/h669ygsrrlno56z)

During this week, we further explain DNA's functions, this time expanding on the process of central dogma. Specifically, we cover transcription, translation, and other aspects of gene expression in biological systems.

### Week 5: [Introduction to Genetic Engineering Techniques](https://canva.link/zicg6p9asmure4b)

The Week 5 lesson is centered on genetic engineering. We first introduce students to bacterial transformation, transfection, and viral vectors, before concluding the lesson with an introduction to CRISPR and its uses in altering or suspending transcription.

### Week 6: [PCR and Gel Electrophoresis](https://canva.link/dyc2k71n5a9tcnr)

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) is a key step in the gene editing process: without it, we would not have a large enough concentration of our target DNA sequences. In this week's lesson, we explain how PCR functions and give students a brief overview of how gel electrophoresis is used to analyze and interpret results.

### Week 7: [Intro to Cloning and Synbio Tools](https://canva.link/3vu0bhzcve8y2jp)

Week 7 focuses on the importance of cloning in synthetic biology, covering the steps taken in cloning and the different ways to purify cloned sequences. Much of this week's lesson is about plasmids—we highlight plasmid design as a key step in the cloning process.

### Week 8: [Exploration of Bioethics](https://canva.link/zgcf61nhli6z88x)

For anyone interested in working in synthetic biology in the future, one of the most important concerns to consider is ethics. This lesson asks students to truly think about whether or not certain experiments are okay to implement, using the five bioethics principles of autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice, and responsibility as a guiding framework.

### Week 9: [CRISPR and Genome Editing](https://canva.link/npw5d4b3crbb50p)

This lesson delves back into CRISPR, focusing this time on both its importance and its limitations. Students conduct a case study to better understand how CRISPR is used throughout synthetic biology today.

### Week 10: [Cell-Free Synthetic Biology](https://canva.link/7ncv6g8peh87i0u)

Week 10 introduces the concept of cell-free synthetic biology as an alternative to traditional in vivo systems. We emphasize that using cell-free systems can substantially reduce waiting time and allow scientists to better control their experiments, but caution students that there are limitations involved as well. We end the lesson with the thought that cell-free is a useful tool in synthetic biology, but there are still circumstances when traditional methods may be superior. It is up to the student to decide what works best for their particular experiment.

### Week 11: [Experimental Design and Data](https://canva.link/p9j6obmycegesdx)

Week 11 is geared towards teaching students how to design experiments, then compile their data in a readable and replicable format. We highlight the importance of control groups as a baseline for experiment validity.

### Week 12: [Your Future in Synthetic Biology](https://canva.link/m4w55bou8bk6fje)

We finalize the FFF semester by introducing potential career options within the field of synthetic biology to our students. We also include an in-depth lesson on SWARM as a real-world example of how the processes we've taught throughout the course can be implemented into a physical synthetic biology project.

By the twelfth week, students are able to take the skills that they have learned throughout the course and apply them towards complex scientific concepts. Throughout the course, we challenge students with complex writing assignments that further expand their knowledge of synthetic biology and build critical thinking. At the end of the course, students are given the opportunity to express their thoughts on the class and give feedback on what we can improve. Many students told us that before taking this course, they had little understanding of synthetic biology and limited interest in science. However, by the end of the course, they discovered not only a genuine interest in science but also a desire to continue studying it in college and potentially pursue it as a career.

Over the past four months, I learned so much, explored new ideas, and gained a deeper understanding of synthetic biology. I am truly grateful for your guidance, support, and dedication. Thank you for inspiring us and making this journey so meaningful."

## Exceptional Science! | SDG 4, 15

The neurodivergent community is often excluded from opportunities to develop and demonstrate higher-level thinking. We are committed to delivering a quality education to our neurodivergent community at Lambert through our Exceptional Science! program. We regularly meet with the neurodivergent class at our school and teach them important science concepts through interactive experiments (see Fig. 3). Such topics include acid-base reactions, genetics, physical reactions, and differences in density (see Fig. 4). We emphasize kinesthetic learning throughout the lessons as a method of anchoring knowledge in physical representations of the concept. Movement is known to help organize attention, support memory, and ease cognitive overload [1].

In our curriculum, some topics show up more often than others. This is because we have identified certain concepts such as genetics, chemical reactions, and physical reactions as being key for understanding complex science. Most of our genetics lessons focus on visualizing the structure of DNA, the composition of cells, and the interactions between organelles. We view reactions as the cornerstone of science as a whole; therefore, we include many examples of reactions and explain the specific processes happening. One reaction type we repeatedly circle back to is acid-base reactions: cell operations are guided by acid-base reactions, and by understanding how they work, students gain a better understanding of the science behind bodily functions. We hope that through this program, our students will gain a better understanding of the science behind their everyday lives.

This year, we also included a game about honey bees and DWV, in which students first act as healthy honey bees and collect "pollen" hidden around the area. In the next round, they simulate having DWV. In this round, students are not allowed to walk, and instead have to hop around to collect the pollen. Through this game, we teach the students the importance of honey bees in our world and emphasize that DWV can destroy colonies. Through Exceptional Science! we aim to link positive memories to scientific topics, empowering students to continue to experiment and learn about the world around them.

## Neurodivergent Handbook | SDG 4, 17

As an extension of the Exceptional Science! program, we updated our Neurodivergent Handbook from the 2025 iGEM season and restructured it into more comprehensive chapters with units. This handbook acts as a guide for educators looking for simple science experiments to conduct with their neurodivergent classes. We added a description of each experiment along with the scientific topics it encompasses to guide educators on what to emphasize during their experiments. Our vision is for this handbook to increase the quality of scientific education for neurodivergent communities across our community, and make the process as simple as possible for teachers.

## Future Plans

We do not want our efforts towards educating the public to end here; instead, we want to expand our resources to more under-represented communities. One organization that we have already started working with is Women in Technology (WIT). Specifically, we are preparing a collaboration with their WIT Girls program, where we will introduce participants to synthetic biology and the scientific research process (SDG 4). Whether it was FFF, conferences, middle school workshops, or elementary school education, our participants’ overwhelmingly positive feedback proved to us that our community wants more. For this we call on future Lambert iGEM teams to fulfill this by making outreach a constant part of every project.
