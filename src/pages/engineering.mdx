# Cycle 1 – Minibinder Creation and Expression



The overall goal of this cycle was to develop and validate a system of expression by producing VP3 protein and de novo minibinders through plasmid cloning, cell-free expression, and Ni-NTA purification.

## Iteration 1.1 – Initial Minibinder Creation

### Design

Our initial approach focused on generating minibinders capable of binding the VP3 P-domain of DWV, potentially interfering with viral attachment to honey bee cells. We utilized existing computational design pipelines, including Mosaic, BindCraft, and BoltzGen, to generate novel minibinder structures and sequences targeting VP3.

### Build

Using the three-dimensional structure of VP3, we generated minibinder candidates through our computational pipelines. We subsequently evaluated these designs using Protenix and Boltz-2, examining interface predicted template modeling scores (ipTM) and interface predicted aligned error (iPAE) to assess predicted structural confidence and binding interface quality.

Based on these evaluations, we selected promising candidates for experimental validation through our partnership with Adaptyv Bio.

### Test

Through our sponsorship with Adaptyv Bio, we experimentally screened our initial minibinder candidates against VP3. Despite favorable computational predictions, none of the tested minibinders demonstrated detectable binding under the assay conditions.

### Learn

The unsuccessful first batch revealed that favorable computational predictions did not necessarily translate into experimental binding. We identified two potential limitations in our design process: epitope selection, which determines where a minibinder interacts with VP3, and solubility, which can influence protein expression and experimental performance.

These findings motivated us to refine our computational workflow by improving hotspot identification and incorporating additional sequence optimization before experimental testing.

## Iteration 1.2 – Epitope Selection and Solubility Optimization

### Design

Following the unsuccessful first batch, we revised our computational design approach to address potential limitations in epitope selection and solubility.

To improve hotspot selection, we incorporated PRINCE, our software that combines computational structural analysis with literature-based evidence to identify promising binding regions (see [Software](/lambert-ga/software)). This allowed us to target alternative epitopes on the VP3 P-domain rather than relying on the initial binding regions.

We also incorporated SolubleMPNN, a protein sequence-design model that favors amino acid sequences with characteristics associated with soluble protein expression. By combining improved epitope selection with sequence optimization, we aimed to generate minibinders with more favorable predicted binding interactions and expression properties.

### Build

Using PRINCE, we identified two new binding hotspots on the VP3 P-domain and generated a second batch of minibinders through our computational design pipelines.

We evaluated the resulting designs using Protenix and Boltz-2, analyzing ipTM and iPAE to assess predicted interface confidence. We additionally used SolubleMPNN to redesign amino acid sequences with the goal of improving solubility while preserving their intended structures and binding interactions. These evaluations allowed us to prioritize promising candidates for a second round of experimental screening.

### Test

We submitted our redesigned minibinder candidates to Adaptyv Bio for experimental binding analysis. Unlike the initial batch, the second batch produced one minibinder with experimentally detectable binding to VP3.

This result demonstrated that our revised computational workflow successfully generated a candidate capable of binding the intended target.

### Learn

The second iteration demonstrated improved experimental outcomes following modifications to epitope selection and sequence optimization. While the initial batch produced no detectable binders, the revised workflow generated a successful VP3-binding minibinder.

These findings highlighted the importance of evaluating not only predicted binding confidence but also the selection of appropriate epitopes and sequence properties relevant to protein expression. Although the improved outcome cannot be attributed to PRINCE or SolubleMPNN independently, the results support the effectiveness of our revised design workflow.

## Iteration 1.3 – Creating a System of Expression

### Design

To establish a reliable production workflow, our goal for this iteration was to develop an expression system in order to validate the efficacy of our de novo minibinders. We based our approach to expression on the pipeline established by Fei et al., 2022 for the expression of the VP3 protein. However, because standard expression protocols often result in low yields, we believed that this would pose problems for our engineered minibinders (Rosano & Ceccarelli, 2014). In order to prevent this, we added a miniprep step to create plasmids encoded for our desired proteins. This allowed us to incorporate a cell-free protein synthesis step to enhance the expression efficacy and ensure sufficient yield for purification (Katzen et al., 2005).

As a result, we decided to apply the following expression protocol:

1. Plasmid Transformation
2. Liquid Inoculation
3. Miniprep
4. Cell-free Expression
5. Ni-NTA Purification
6. Dot-Blot Analysis

To validate our protein production framework prior to minibinder experimentation, the green fluorescent protein (GFP) and its complementary anti-GFP nanobody were selected to serve as a model system and positive control.

Negative controls included samples containing standalone GFP and nanobody to establish baseline fluorescence and verify binding specificity. We expected that the GFP-nanobody complex would increase fluorescence emission as compared to GFP alone, confirming our expression and binding detection system works before transitioning to VP3 minibinders (Kirchhofer et al., 2010).

# Modeling

## Epidemiological Model

We simulated a single honeybee colony day by day over five years under identical weather conditions, comparing an untreated colony with one receiving sucrose syrup containing dsRNA from the beginning. Because the model assumes a continuous supply of fresh syrup, feeding schedules and treatment timing were not variables we needed to optimize. This allowed us to focus on two central questions: how effectively does the treatment control Varroa mites and deformed wing virus over five years, and how reliable are the model's predictions?

Developing a model we could confidently defend required four rounds of revisions, with each version revealing limitations in the one before it. We retained these earlier versions to document how the model changed as we identified and addressed problems with our initial assumptions. Each revision improved our understanding of the system and helped us build a model whose results we could evaluate more critically.

### Iteration 1 – The first working model

#### Design

The model follows nine quantities through each day. Adult bees are split into those carrying deformed wing virus and those without it, alongside a running total of the ones the virus has killed, and the mites are divided much the same way, into those alive and able to feed and the subset of those carrying virus they can pass on. The treated colony adds three more quantities that stay at zero in the untreated run.

| Population | What it counts |
|:-----------|:---------------|
| Healthy adults | Bees with no deformed wing virus |
| Infected adults | Bees carrying the virus, which shortens their lives |
| Virus deaths | Running total of bees the virus has killed |
| Protected bees | Bees currently holding enough dsRNA to matter |
| Feeding mites | Mites alive and able to feed |
| Virus-carrying mites | The share of those mites that can pass the virus on |
| Disabled mites | Mites that fed on a protected bee and can no longer feed |
| Mites killed | Running total of mites the treatment has removed |

Control and treatment are not two separate models. They are the same equations run twice over the same weather, and the only thing that differs between the two runs is whether syrup is available.

#### Build

The first version kept every rate constant, with no seasons and no weather at all. A single bite rate carried the virus from mites to bees, infected bees were allowed to recover, and deaths from the virus came out of a term of their own. We drew the results as four separate figures, one population per figure, which seemed reasonable at the time.

#### Test

It ran, and the treated colony did better than the untreated one, which was encouraging for about a day. Underneath, three things were wrong.

The bite rate was doing two jobs at once, setting both how often mites fed on bees and how often the virus actually moved between them, so there was no way to change one without dragging the other along with it. Recovery was worse, because we had infected adults returning to health at a steady rate and nothing in the deformed wing virus literature we were citing supports that. The virus death term had been written as the total death rate of an infected bee rather than the extra death the virus causes, so ordinary mortality and virus mortality were quietly counted against each other, which also forced an awkward constraint where the virus term had to come out larger than the baseline one for the arithmetic to behave.

On top of all that, four populations on four separate axes meant nobody could put the treated and untreated colonies next to each other and see the difference.

There is no seasonal cycle anywhere in these four, because there were no seasons yet, so the populations settle into flat equilibria instead of the yearly rise and fall a real colony goes through.

#### Learn

The model was revised to represent transmission from mites to bees and virus acquisition from bees to mites as separate rates. This also allows mites to clear the virus rather than remain infected indefinitely. The virus related death rate was redefined as the additional daily mortality caused by infection. As a result, infected bees experience both baseline mortality and mortality caused by the virus, ensuring that infection increases their daily risk of death. This change also resolved the constraint identified in the previous model.

Recovery remains included in the equations but is set to zero throughout the year. This assumption is stated explicitly so that readers can evaluate its justification and consider alternative values. Retaining the recovery term also makes the modeling decision transparent rather than removing the process from the model entirely.

### Iteration 2 – Seasons and weather

#### Design

A model with constant rates cannot adequately represent the seasonal dynamics of Varroa mites and deformed wing virus, particularly because winter survival is a major factor in colony mortality. To account for these changes, thirteen rates were assigned four quarterly values corresponding to the beginning of January, April, July, and October. The values were connected using a smooth curve that repeats annually. Weather was also incorporated to represent seasonal variation in forage availability and environmental conditions, for which direct data were unavailable.

#### Build

The weather went in as four independent random series, one each for brood rearing, mites, bee survival and contact between the two, with each series carrying its own persistence, shock size and event scale. This pass also added most of the structure the model still has today: saturating gates so a small colony cannot rear brood or support mite reproduction efficiently, logistic mite growth against a capacity measured in mites per bee, a direct cost to adults from the mites feeding on them, crowding deaths once the colony outgrows its space, grooming, and a rule declaring a colony dead once it drops below a few hundred adults.

#### Test

Five problems were identified in this version of the model, all through reviewing the equations rather than examining the simulation output.

First, weather was held constant throughout each day. Multiplying a smooth seasonal curve by this daily step function caused the right hand side of the differential equation to jump at midnight. This made the function discontinuous in time and required the solver to use one day time steps to handle these changes.

Second, two saturation constants had incorrectly defined weather responses. Increasing a half saturation constant reduces brood care and mite reproduction, so assigning these constants positive weather sensitivities meant that favorable weather reduced brood care instead of increasing it.

Third, the restriction on mite growth was applied to the entire logistic expression rather than only to mite births. Consequently, when the colony began to decline, mite removal also decreased, allowing mite numbers to increase without adequate control.

Fourth, the model used four independent weather series. These could not represent a poor summer in which brood rearing conditions deteriorated while conditions for mite reproduction remained favorable. Without a shared weather mechanism, the model could not adequately represent this combination of conditions.

Finally, the brood term reduced emergence through an exponential decay function based on the number of virus carrying mites. Its rate constant had no measurable biological counterpart in the available evidence, leaving this part of the model without a clear empirical justification.

  caption="Honeybee and Varroa control groups, where both populations collapsed."

This version introduces seasonal variation for the first time, but also reveals a major problem: mite populations continue to rise instead of stabilizing, eventually causing the colony to decline.

#### Learn

Three of these problems stemmed from the same underlying issue: incorrect biological assumptions within the model. This highlights a limitation of mathematical modeling rather than coding, since an equation can be dimensionally correct and numerically stable while still misrepresenting biological processes. An incorrect sign on a saturation constant does not produce an error message, but instead reverses the effect of weather throughout the simulation. Similarly, applying a growth restriction to the wrong part of a logistic term removes the limits on mite growth when the colony is most vulnerable.

Reviewing each equation and evaluating its biological implications identified all four problems. However, running the simulation and examining its graphs revealed none of them. This is an important lesson for other teams: model validation requires checking the biological meaning of the equations, not just examining the simulation output.

### Iteration 3 – Fixing the equations and re-sourcing the parameters

#### Design

Five changes, each aimed at one of the problems above, plus a pass back through every single parameter to check it against the source it was supposed to have come from.

#### Build

1. The weather driver is now interpolated between daily values, so every rate is continuous in time. The daily values themselves are unchanged and only the values in between are new.
2. The two saturating constants take negative weather sensitivities, while the crowding threshold keeps a positive one, because more forage genuinely does let a colony hold more bees before crowding starts to cost it anything.
3. Mite births are gated by colony size and density removal is not, since mites can only breed where there is capped brood but crowding kills them whether or not any brood exists.
4. A single shared weather index now drives all four groups, each through its own loading, so a bad summer hits brood rearing, bee survival, mite conditions and virus spread together. Collapsing four series into one also removed about twenty numbers we had no source for.
5. The brood term became an infested-cell calculation rather than an exponential decay. Cells entered per day comes from the invasion rate and the number of virus-carrying mites, cells capped per day is the potential emergence, and the emergence lost from an infested cell is a measured quantity. Same curve shape as before, but every piece of it can now be quoted.

Fifteen parameter values changed in the same pass. A few had been placeholders nobody ever went back to, several came from sources that said something slightly different once we read them properly rather than skimming them, and one was a flat number where the paper it came from had actually given a seasonal one. The script now records the previous value next to each change, so nothing is hidden and any of it can be argued with. One parameter left entirely, since the old brood-suppression rate had no measurable counterpart and was replaced by the infested-cell calculation above, taking a dozen weather numbers with it when the four series became one.

#### Test

The fifth change also clarified a limitation in the parameter estimation. Because coverage stabilizes at a fixed ratio of uptake to loss, potency, uptake rate, decay rate, and the two dose response parameters affect the data through a single product. As a result, these parameters cannot be fitted independently. Increasing the decay rate can be almost entirely offset by increasing potency.

#### Learn

Only one parameter in the treatment model can be calibrated using the available data: potency. The uptake rate, dsRNA loss rate, and death rate of disabled mites can each be measured independently, while the two dose response shape parameters are fixed at published values. Potency is therefore the only remaining parameter that can be estimated from treatment data. The script calibrates it using a mite reduction value near the midpoint of the range reported in field trials of RNAi treatments for Varroa.

The calibration routine also accounts for a limitation identified during testing. If the untreated colony collapses before the simulation ends, its mite population remains at zero for the rest of the simulation. Calculating the average mite reduction across the entire period can therefore underestimate the treatment effect or even produce a negative value. To avoid this, all reported treatment effects are calculated only over the days when both colonies remain alive.

### Iteration 4 – Checking the result from outside

#### Design

Until this point, the model had been evaluated using a single script, one weather sequence, and the same team's review. This left three main limitations: the results had not been tested across different weather conditions, the one at a time sensitivity analysis covered only the six treatment parameters, and the model had not been independently rederived.

#### Build

So the model was rebuilt in Python, working from the equations rather than translating the MATLAB line by line, then checked against a GNU Octave run of the original. On top of that we ran a global analysis: a thousand Latin hypercube draws across every input in the model, with fresh weather for each draw, scored by partial rank correlation under a strict significance cut-off. Then six stress tests, each pushing one property at a time well past anything we would call nominal.

#### Test

The independent validation confirmed that the model was consistent across implementations. Under fixed weather conditions, both implementations agreed to five decimal places, and the variation across random weather sequences was statistically indistinguishable. Halving the time step produced no meaningful changes in the results, and running the model in an older Python version with older libraries produced identical outputs.

The global analysis revealed a clearer picture of the results. Across 1,000 simulations, both colonies survived in approximately 80% of cases. In these simulations, the treatment reduced feeding mites by around 96%, with the lowest reduction still exceeding 84%. The analysis also showed that the two main outcomes of interest are influenced by different factors, which was not apparent beforehand.

| Outcome | What actually drives it |
|:--------|:------------------------|
| Fewer feeding mites | The potency of our dsRNA, by a wide margin, then the adult death rate and the emergence rate |
| More adult bees | The adult death rate, the mite growth rate and the natural mite death rate, in that order |

None of the six treatment parameters moves the bee gain by a significant amount.

#### Learn

The results suggest that mite reduction depends primarily on the dsRNA treatment, while the difference in adult bee populations is influenced more by the colony's own population dynamics. This distinction is important because the treatment does not directly determine the number of bees in the colony. A higher adult mortality rate reduces the size of the untreated colony, which also limits the number of mites it can support. As a result, the difference between the treated and untreated colonies becomes smaller. The treated colony's mite population changes very little across these conditions because most of its total mite days occur during the first year, with few mites remaining afterward.

The stress tests also show that the treatment remains effective across a wide range of conditions. A 90% reduction in mites is maintained even when uptake falls to one quarter of the planned level, dsRNA degrades several times faster than measured, and potency decreases by nearly half. The effect declines substantially only when potency falls to one fifth of its nominal value, at which point the reduction drops to approximately 50%.

For beekeepers, one of the most important findings is the difference in colony survival. Across 1,000 simulations, approximately one fifth of untreated colonies collapsed, most during the second spring. In comparison, only one treated colony collapsed. No treated colonies collapsed in any of the six stress tests.
